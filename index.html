About

I've always had a burning need to understand where the world came from, how it works, and where it is heading. Growing up, that meant absorbing everything — newspapers, television, books, the internet, social media, podcasts and so on. Newspapers gave us a daily record of the world. Television let us watch it as it happened. Podcasts let anyone go deep on anything. Social media handed the audience a microphone. Each layer moved the public closer to the information. But none of them let people price what they believe.

Prediction markets are where that gap closes for me. Take a question like "Will there be an Ebola pandemic in 2026?" A headline about an outbreak is written to be read. A viral post is written to be shared. Neither pays a price for being wrong. But when someone places a bet on that question, they have to weigh everything they've read against what they're willing to lose — and the market price that emerges is a signal none of the other layers can produce: what people actually believe, with money behind it. That's not a replacement for existing media — it's a new layer on top of it, one that lets society collectively build a more accurate picture of the world.

The question I'm sitting with now: understanding where existing prediction markets like Polymarket and Kalshi fall short, and whether it's a gap I can solve.

Passion for Crypto

What struck me most is that Ethereum & Bitcoin aren't just financial networks. They're self-governing systems — networks that organize resources, coordinate strangers, and evolve their own rules without central control. The idea that you can align stakeholders and users by design, rather than by institution, is the most interesting organizational primitive I've encountered. Most people see prices. I see a new way of organizing human effort, still in its earliest chapters.

Books

Books I return to.

01
Understanding Media — Marshall McLuhan

Every technology is an extension of some human organ or faculty: the gun extends the hand, the camera extends the eye, electricity extends the nervous system. And every extension reshapes us in turn: our biological and social wiring adapts to whatever we've built, whether we notice it or not. McLuhan's core claim, "the medium is the message," is that a new medium creates an environment, and it's the environment that shapes us, not the content inside it. The railway accelerated movement and gave rise to new kinds of cities; the phone accelerated access to the internet and gave rise to new kinds of knowledge workers; the internet itself accelerated the sharing of knowledge, information, and communication. What actually affects us isn't the content that flows through a medium, it's the new environment the technology enables.

02
The Social Contract — Jean-Jacques Rousseau

Rousseau's answer to how people can remain free while living under a state is the social contract: citizens unite their separate powers through a kind of covenant, agreeing to govern themselves as a collective and protect one another's rights. Forming a nation means giving up certain freedoms from the state of nature, but it replaces them with the far more valuable "civil freedom" of society, which lets people develop themselves morally and rationally. A state is only legitimate when the people themselves hold sovereignty: their job is to make the laws and delegate the power to carry them out to a government, while keeping a careful watch to ensure that government never seizes power for itself.

03
Scale — Geoffrey West

West introduces the concept of complexity science and scaling laws, and finds that mammals are essentially scaled versions of one another: know an animal's size and these laws tell you its heart rate, how much it eats, how long it lives. This regularity traces back to the fractal geometry of the networks that supply energy and distribute resources inside the body. The bolder move was showing that cities and companies are built like biological organisms, constellations of the same kind of networks. The laws that govern the cell can be scaled up to the city and the company, all of them following the same simple, powerful rules.

04
Imagined Communities — Benedict Anderson

Anderson argues that nations must be understood as imagined communities, because they are simply too large for any member to actually know all the others. A nation exists only in the sense that each member imagines a relationship with everyone else across time and space. But being imagined doesn't make it any less real: nations are real enough that people are prepared to kill and die for them.

05
Debt: The First 5,000 Years — David Graeber

Graeber traces the history of debt across five thousand years and dismantles the traditional myth that money began with barter, that people traded goods directly, found it clumsy, and invented coins to smooth it over. Drawing on anthropological evidence, he argues the sequence ran the other way: credit and debt came thousands of years before coinage, and coins themselves emerged from warfare and state extraction. His central example is the cycle that provisioned ancient armies. An empire mints coins stamped with the ruler's face and pays them to its soldiers as wages. It then decrees that every peasant and shopkeeper must pay taxes in those same coins. The villagers never needed the coins to live, since they already ran on informal credit and reciprocal obligation, but now they have to acquire them or face punishment, and the only people holding coins are the soldiers stationed nearby. So they sell food, armour, horses, and labour to the army in exchange for coin, then hand that coin back to the treasury as tax, where it is paid out again for the next campaign. In one move the state turns its entire population into a decentralized supply chain for its military, without ever shipping a single sack of grain to the front. What emerges is a view of money not as a commodity with intrinsic worth but as a standardized IOU, a token of debt to society made valuable because the sovereign demands it back, and of debt itself as a force that has shaped social relations, power, and the moral fabric of societies throughout history.
